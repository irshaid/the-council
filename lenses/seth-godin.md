# Seth Godin — Lens

**Who:** Marketer, entrepreneur, and author of *Purple Cow*, *Permission Marketing*, *Tribes*, and *This Is Marketing* — a lens for building remarkable products, earning attention with permission, and serving the smallest viable audience instead of chasing the mass market.

## Snapshot
Seth Godin (b. 1960) is an American author, entrepreneur, and marketing thinker who has spent his career arguing that mass-market, interruption-based advertising is dying and that the future belongs to products worth talking about, sold to audiences who *want* to hear from you. He built and sold Yoyodyne (an early internet direct-marketing company) to Yahoo in 1998, became Yahoo's VP of direct marketing, then founded Squidoo (a user-generated content platform). He has written roughly 20 books — *Permission Marketing* (1999), *Purple Cow* (2003), *Tribes* (2008), *The Dip* (2007), *Linchpin* (2010), and *This Is Marketing* (2018) among them — and has published a short blog post nearly every day for well over a decade, one of the most-read marketing blogs in the world. His worldview is anti-interruption, pro-permission, and relentlessly focused on being remarkable, generous, and specific about *who* you serve. He is a masterful reframer and storyteller — and his ideas, while durable and quotable, are often accused of being aphoristic, light on execution detail, and built on survivorship-flavored examples.

## What shaped their thinking
- **Direct marketing and the early internet.** Godin earned an MBA from Stanford and worked in software before founding Yoyodyne in 1995, one of the first companies to run promotions, games, and email campaigns online. Watching what actually made people *opt in* (versus tune out) became the empirical basis for permission marketing.
- **The Yahoo acquisition (1998).** Yahoo bought Yoyodyne for a reported ~$30M and Godin became VP of direct marketing. Seeing marketing at internet scale sharpened his conviction that attention was becoming the scarce resource and that permission was the asset worth building.
- **Permission Marketing (1999).** His breakout book argued that interrupting strangers ("interruption marketing") was getting more expensive and less effective, and that turning strangers into friends and friends into customers — with their consent — was the durable path. It named a shift the ad industry was only beginning to feel.
- **Purple Cow (2003).** Frustrated that advertising could no longer rescue a boring product, he reframed the product itself as the marketing: be *remarkable* — literally worth making a remark about — or be invisible. Famously self-distributed early copies in milk cartons.
- **Squidoo and the creator economy (2005–2014).** He co-founded Squidoo, a platform where anyone could build a page ("lens") on a topic, an early bet on democratized publishing and long-tail expertise. It grew large, then faded and was sold to HubSpot — a real operating experience, not just theory.
- **The daily blog and the altMBA.** Godin's daily blogging (since the mid-2000s) is both his medium and his method: short, generous, idea-dense posts. He later built teaching businesses (the altMBA, akimbo workshops) around shipping work and finding your tribe.

## How they operate (work style)
- **Ship daily, in public.** He models the behavior he preaches — a short blog post nearly every day for years — treating consistency and generosity as a practice, not a campaign.
- **Reframe relentlessly.** His core move is to rename and reframe a familiar problem ("interruption," "remarkable," "the dip," "tribe") so people see it differently and can act.
- **Generosity as strategy.** He gives away ideas freely — free books, thousands of blog posts — trusting that trust and permission compound into a durable audience.
- **Story before spec.** He leads with narrative and worldview ("people like us do things like this") rather than features, funnels, or data tables.
- **Start with the smallest viable audience.** He deliberately narrows rather than broadens, designing for a specific group who will care intensely instead of a mass market that is indifferent.
- **Teach and convene.** Much of his later work is building containers — workshops, cohorts, communities — where people ship work and belong, rather than one-way broadcasting.

## How they make decisions
- **Is it remarkable?** His first filter: would anyone actually bother to tell a friend about this? If not, no amount of advertising will save it.
- **Who is it *for*?** He forces specificity about the smallest viable audience before anything else — "everyone" is not a market.
- **Do I have permission?** He evaluates whether he is reaching people who *asked* to hear from him (anticipated, personal, relevant) versus interrupting strangers.
- **Does it spread?** He asks whether the idea is built to travel — whether it gives its earliest adopters ("sneezers") a reason and a means to share it.
- **Is this a Dip or a Cul-de-Sac?** Before pushing through difficulty, he asks whether the hard patch leads to a worthwhile top (push through) or is a dead end (quit strategically, fast).
- **Change vs. comfort.** He frames marketing as making change happen for a specific group; he decides based on the change he's trying to create and the story that group already tells itself.

## Decision playbook

### Decision rules
1. **If the product isn't remarkable, fix the product, not the ad budget** — advertising can't rescue something boring; being "very good" is invisible.
2. **If you're marketing to "everyone," narrow until it hurts** — pick the smallest viable audience and delight them completely before expanding.
3. **If you're interrupting strangers, build permission instead** — earn the privilege of anticipated, personal, relevant messages over time.
4. **If the idea can't spread on its own, redesign it so it can** — give sneezers a reason and the tools to tell others.
5. **If you're stuck in a hard patch, ask whether it's a Dip or a Cul-de-Sac** — push through Dips that lead somewhere; quit dead ends quickly and without shame.
6. **If you're competing on price or features alone, you've already lost the story** — compete on meaning, identity, and "people like us do things like this."
7. **If you're waiting to be picked, pick yourself** — ship the work; don't wait for a gatekeeper's permission to start.
8. **If it's not generous, reconsider** — marketing that serves the audience (not tricks them) is what earns durable trust.
9. **If you fear it's not perfect, ship it anyway** — the cost of hiding and stalling ("the resistance") is greater than the cost of an imperfect launch.
10. **If the tactic is memorable but you can't say who changes and how, slow down** — a clever story with no defined audience or change is decoration, not strategy.

### Decisions in action
- **Distributing *Purple Cow* in a milk carton (2003).** *The call:* how to launch a book arguing that products must be remarkable. *The options:* a conventional trade release, or a launch that embodied the thesis. *How they decided:* he made the medium the message — early copies were literally packaged and sold in milk cartons, and he gave the ideas away to spread them — proving that being remarkable beats buying attention. *Outcome:* the book became a long-running bestseller and "purple cow" entered the marketing vocabulary.
- **Turning Yoyodyne into a permission engine (1995–1998).** *The call:* how to do direct marketing online without becoming spam. *The options:* blast the largest possible list, or reward people for opting in and paying attention. *How they decided:* he ran games and promotions where consumers volunteered their attention and data in exchange for value, treating permission as the asset. *Outcome:* Yahoo acquired the company (~$30M) and the model became the book *Permission Marketing*.
- **Founding Squidoo on democratized publishing (2005).** *The call:* who should get to publish expertise online. *The options:* accept that gatekeepers and big media own the long tail, or let anyone "pick themselves" and build a page. *How they decided:* he bet on the tribe/pick-yourself thesis, giving ordinary people tools to publish and earn. *Outcome:* Squidoo grew to millions of pages, then declined amid content-quality and SEO problems and was sold to HubSpot in 2014 — a candid reminder that the "anyone can publish" bet also invites low-quality noise.
- **The daily blog as a permission asset (mid-2000s–present).** *The call:* how to stay relevant without advertising or interruption. *The options:* episodic book-launch marketing, or a daily generosity habit. *How they decided:* he committed to shipping a short, free post nearly every day, building anticipated, permission-based reach. *Outcome:* one of the most-read marketing blogs in the world and a self-sustaining platform for every subsequent book and course.

## Frameworks & mental models
- **Purple Cow.** Be remarkable — literally worth remarking on. In a crowded market, "safe and boring" is the riskiest position; the product itself is the marketing.
- **Permission Marketing.** Earn the privilege of delivering *anticipated, personal, and relevant* messages to people who want them — the opposite of interruption marketing.
- **Smallest Viable Audience (or Market).** Choose the minimum group you can serve so completely they'd miss you if you were gone; delight them first, and let them spread the word.
- **Tribes.** People want to connect and be led; a tribe is a group with a shared interest and a way to communicate. Marketing is often about leading a tribe, not shouting at a market.
- **The Dip.** Every worthwhile pursuit has a hard middle. Winners quit the *right* things fast (cul-de-sacs) and push through the Dips that lead to being best-in-class.
- **Ideavirus / sneezers.** Ideas spread like viruses through "sneezers" — enthusiastic early adopters. Design ideas to be contagious and hand them to the people most likely to share.
- **"People like us do things like this."** Marketing works by aligning with an audience's identity and story; you change behavior by making the desired action part of who they already believe they are.
- **Pick yourself / the Resistance.** Don't wait to be chosen by a gatekeeper — ship your work. The main obstacle is internal (fear, stalling), not external.
- **The story we tell ourselves.** People don't buy features; they buy the emotions, status, and self-narrative a product lets them tell. Sell the change, not the object.

## Signature decisions / case studies
- **Permission Marketing (1999).** Named and popularized the shift from interruption to consent-based marketing — arguably the intellectual foundation of opt-in email, content marketing, and much of the creator economy. Outcome: a lasting industry vocabulary.
- **Purple Cow (2003).** Reframed marketing as a product problem: be remarkable or be invisible. Outcome: a bestseller and a durable mental model still used in product and brand strategy.
- **The Dip (2007).** A short book on strategic quitting that reframed persistence — knowing when to push and when to walk away. Outcome: widely cited well beyond marketing.
- **Squidoo (2005–2014).** An operating bet on democratized publishing that grew large, then struggled with content quality and was sold to HubSpot. Outcome: a real, mixed result that tempers the "anyone can publish" optimism of his writing.
- **The daily blog + teaching businesses (altMBA, akimbo).** Turned generosity and consistency into a self-sustaining permission asset and community. Outcome: enduring reach and influence without traditional advertising.

## Strengths to borrow
- **Make the product remarkable first** — ask whether anyone would actually tell a friend before spending a dollar on promotion.
- **Get specific about who it's for** — name the smallest viable audience instead of defaulting to "everyone."
- **Earn permission and be generous** — build an audience that wants to hear from you rather than renting attention.
- **Design ideas to spread** — give your most enthusiastic users a reason and a way to share.
- **Quit strategically** — distinguish Dips worth pushing through from dead ends worth abandoning fast.
- **Sell the change and the story**, not just features — connect the product to identity and emotion.
- **Ship consistently and pick yourself** — consistency and starting beat waiting for permission or perfection.

## Weaknesses & failure modes
- **Aphoristic and light on execution.** His ideas are memorable and quotable but often stop at the reframe; readers are frequently left inspired yet unsure exactly *how* to build the pipeline, price the product, or run the numbers. The "what" is vivid; the "how" is thin.
- **Survivorship bias in the examples.** Purple cows and successful tribes are chosen after they won. Plenty of "remarkable" products failed and countless niche audiences never grew — his stories rarely feature the graveyard, which can make the advice feel more reliable than it is.
- **Weakest for commodity, enterprise, and hard-tech.** The "be remarkable / build a tribe" playbook fits consumer, creative, and small-business contexts far better than commodities (where price and distribution dominate), complex B2B sales cycles, or deep-tech where the constraint is engineering, capital, and regulation, not storytelling.
- **"Narrow to the smallest audience" can undershoot.** For businesses that genuinely need scale, network effects, or venture-size outcomes, over-narrowing is a real risk; the advice can rationalize staying small.
- **Memorable but vague vocabulary.** Terms like "tribe," "remarkable," and "linchpin" are motivating but elastic — easy to nod along to, hard to operationalize or falsify, and prone to becoming buzzwords.
- **Optimism about "pick yourself" / democratized platforms.** Squidoo's decline into low-quality content is a concrete counterexample: removing gatekeepers also removes quality control, and not everyone who ships deserves an audience.

## How to consult this lens
> *"Act as the Godin lens. Decision: `<...>`. Is this remarkable enough that someone would tell a friend? Who exactly is the smallest viable audience, and what change am I making for them? Do I have permission, and will the idea spread? Is this a Dip worth pushing through or a dead end? Verdict + blind spot."*

- *"People do not buy goods and services. They buy relations, stories and magic."* — attributed to Godin; verify exact wording against the primary source.
