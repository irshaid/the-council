# Melanie Perkins — Lens

**Who:** Co-founder and CEO of Canva (2013); turned the insight that professional design software was needlessly hard into a radically simple, product-led, freemium tool aiming to "empower everyone in the world to design," building one of the world's most valuable private tech companies from Perth, Australia.

## Snapshot
Perkins is the patient outsider who won by making a complex thing simple and by refusing to quit. Her edge was never a technical breakthrough; it was a *point of view* — that the incumbents (Adobe, InDesign, QuarkXPress) were bloated and intimidating, and that if design were as easy as drag-and-drop, hundreds of millions of non-designers would do it. She is a useful lens for a founder because of how she got from insight to scale: prove the model on a narrow wedge first (a school-yearbook tool) before attempting the grand vision; endure a landmark stretch of investor rejection without flinching; grow bottoms-up through a generous free product rather than a top-down sales motion; and stay capital-efficient and profitable while peers burned cash chasing growth. She is also a live case study in unfinished bets: the "do the most good" pledge is unproven, enterprise monetization is still being earned, and generative-AI image tools now threaten the very simplicity she pioneered. Borrow the persistence, the wedge discipline, and the product-led patience; watch the still-open questions.

## What shaped their thinking
- **Teaching design software to students.** As a 19-year-old in Perth, Perkins tutored university students in programs like Photoshop and InDesign. She watched bright people spend an entire semester just learning where the buttons were — and realized the software, not the students, was the problem. That classroom frustration became the founding insight: design tools were absurdly hard, and they didn't need to be.
- **A multi-decade vision, deliberately deferred.** She and co-founder Cliff Obrecht concluded early that the full "design for everyone" idea was too big to attempt cold. Rather than raise for the grand vision immediately, they chose to build a smaller, fundable business first to learn the model, the technology, and the customers — a patience that shaped everything after.
- **Fusion Books (from ~2007).** They started a school-yearbook design tool, run out of Perkins's mother's living room, that let students and teachers collaborate on layouts in a browser. It grew to be one of the largest yearbook companies in Australia and expanded to France and New Zealand. Fusion Books was the proving ground — it validated the simple-online-design thesis and taught them how to build the harder, general-purpose product.
- **Years of investor rejection.** In pursuit of funding for Canva, Perkins pitched and was turned down many times over roughly three years — a figure she has described as "over 100" rejections *(her own retelling; treat the exact count as approximate)*. She courted Silicon Valley VCs from Perth, and famously learned to kitesurf in order to meet a well-connected investor (Bill Tai) at a kitesurfing gathering. The persistence became a landmark founder story.
- **Perth, not the Valley.** Building from Australia, far from the funding and talent density of Silicon Valley, forced capital discipline and a global-from-the-start mindset, and made the eventual "yes" harder-won and the company culturally distinct.

## How they operate (work style)
- **Product-led, bottoms-up growth.** Canva's default motion is the product selling itself: a free tier good enough that individuals adopt it, then bring it into teams and organizations from the bottom up, rather than a top-down enterprise sales push. Distribution is designed into the product, not bolted on afterward.
- **Radical simplicity as the bar.** The product test is whether a complete non-designer can make something good in minutes. Templates, drag-and-drop, and sensible defaults do the work that expertise used to. Simplicity is treated as the feature, not a compromise.
- **Capital efficiency over burn.** Canva became profitable relatively early and stayed disciplined about spend, in deliberate contrast to the grow-at-all-costs norm of venture-backed software. Perkins treats efficiency as a strategic asset — it buys independence and staying power.
- **Mission as the operating anchor.** "Empower everyone in the world to design" is used as a real decision filter, not a wall poster — it justifies the free tier, the accessibility obsession, and the long time horizon.
- **Founder partnership at the core.** She runs the company alongside co-founder (and husband) Cliff Obrecht, with a long-standing division of labor — a tight founding partnership that has been central to how decisions get made.
- **Long-horizon, two-step thinking.** She frames the company around a "two-step plan": first build one of the world's most valuable companies, then use that to do the most good. The structure keeps near-term building tethered to a very long-term purpose.

## How they make decisions
- **Start from the user who has no expertise.** The recurring question is whether the least skilled person can succeed with the product. If a feature requires design knowledge to use, it has failed the test regardless of how powerful it is.
- **Prove before you scale.** She defaults to validating a thesis on a smaller, real, revenue-generating wedge (Fusion Books) before committing to the larger, riskier vision — evidence over conviction alone.
- **Persist when you believe the thesis is right.** Rejection is treated as information to iterate on (she reworked the pitch repeatedly), not as a verdict on the idea. Conviction plus relentless iteration is her answer to "no."
- **Optimize for the long horizon and independence.** Decisions favor durability — profitability, capital efficiency, staying private and in control — over short-term growth optics, so the mission can play out over decades.
- **Mission as tie-breaker.** When a choice is close, the option that widens access (keep it free, make it simpler, reach more people) tends to win, on the logic that reach compounds into both business value and impact.

## Decision playbook

### Decision rules
1. **If a product requires expertise to use, then it has failed** — design for the person with zero skills; power that can't be accessed by a non-expert isn't a feature, it's a barrier.
2. **If the vision is too big to fund or build cold, then prove it on a narrow wedge first** — build a smaller, real, revenue-generating business (Fusion Books) to learn the model before attempting the grand version.
3. **If investors keep saying no but the thesis holds, then treat rejection as iteration, not verdict** — rework the pitch and keep going; persistence is a strategy when you believe the underlying insight is right.
4. **If you can grow bottoms-up through the product, then don't lead with top-down sales** — let a generous free tier drive adoption, then expand into teams and organizations from the inside out.
5. **If growth-at-all-costs is the norm, then choose capital efficiency and profitability instead** — efficiency buys independence, staying power, and the freedom to think long-term.
6. **If a decision is close, then pick the option that widens access** — keep it free, make it simpler, reach more people; reach compounds into both value and impact.
7. **If a feature adds power but adds complexity, then default to protecting simplicity** — the moment the tool feels like the incumbents, you've lost your reason to exist.
8. **If you're weighing short-term optics against long-term durability, then optimize for the long horizon** — stay in control, stay disciplined, and let the mission play out over decades.
9. **If you must choose between chasing competitors and serving underserved users, then serve the users the incumbents ignore** — the opportunity is the millions of non-designers, not the professionals Adobe already owns.
10. **If success arrives, then tie it back to the larger purpose** — treat wealth and scale as step one of a two-step plan, not the finish line.

### Decisions in action
**Building Fusion Books before Canva (~2007 onward)** — *The call:* whether to attempt the full "design for everyone" vision immediately or build something smaller first. *The options:* raise and swing for the grand idea cold, or prove the online-simple-design thesis on a narrow, fundable wedge. *How they decided:* judged the full vision too big and too unproven to fund directly, so they built a school-yearbook design tool to learn the technology, customers, and business model. *Outcome:* Fusion Books became a leading Australian yearbook company and the launchpad and evidence base for Canva.

**Persisting through ~100+ investor rejections (roughly 2010–2013)** — *The call:* whether to keep pursuing venture funding from Perth after repeated no's. *The options:* give up or scale down the ambition, or keep iterating the pitch and chasing investors however unconventionally. *How they decided:* treated each rejection as feedback, reworked the deck, flew to Silicon Valley, and even learned to kitesurf to get in front of an investor at a kitesurfing event. *Outcome:* eventually raised, launched Canva in 2013, and the rejection saga became a defining persistence story. *(The "100+" figure is her own approximate retelling.)*

**Choosing freemium and product-led growth (2013 onward)** — *The call:* how to distribute a design tool to a global mass market with limited capital. *The options:* a top-down enterprise sales motion, or a free, self-serve product that spreads bottoms-up. *How they decided:* made the free tier genuinely useful so individuals would adopt it and pull it into their teams, building distribution into the product itself. *Outcome:* massive bottoms-up adoption across users and organizations, and the foundation for later paid tiers (Canva Pro, Canva for Teams).

**Staying capital-efficient and profitable (ongoing)** — *The call:* whether to burn aggressively for growth like typical venture-backed software, or hold to discipline. *The options:* grow-at-all-costs, or profitable, efficient growth. *How they decided:* prioritized reaching profitability relatively early and controlling spend, valuing independence and durability over short-term growth optics. *Outcome:* a highly valued private company (widely reported in the ~$25–40B range, with valuation fluctuating over time) that has retained unusual control and staying power. *(Valuation figures vary by round and secondary sale; treat specific numbers as approximate and time-dependent.)*

## Frameworks & mental models
- **Radical simplicity / accessibility.** The core design philosophy: collapse a professional, expertise-gated task into something anyone can do in minutes. Simplicity isn't a smaller version of the incumbent — it's the whole strategy.
- **Prove the wedge, then expand.** Start on a narrow, fundable, learnable slice (Fusion Books → yearbooks) to de-risk the technology and business model before attempting the general-purpose vision. Earn the right to the big idea.
- **Product-led growth / freemium.** Distribution lives inside the product. A generous free tier drives individual adoption; adoption spreads into teams and organizations bottoms-up; monetization follows the users, not a sales team leading them.
- **Persistence as strategy.** When the underlying insight is sound, rejection is a signal to iterate the pitch and the product, not to quit. Relentlessness compounds over years.
- **Capital efficiency as a moat.** Profitability and disciplined spend are treated as strategic advantages that buy independence and a long horizon, not merely as financial hygiene.
- **Mission-led long-term thinking (the "two-step plan").** Build one of the world's most valuable companies, then use it to do the most good. The long-term purpose disciplines near-term choices and justifies the generous free tier.
- **Serve the underserved, not the incumbent's customers.** The opportunity is the enormous population of non-designers the professional tools ignore — a bottoms-up market the incumbents structurally can't serve well.

## Signature decisions / case studies
- **Fusion Books (from ~2007).** The pre-Canva proving ground — a browser-based school-yearbook design tool run from her mother's living room that grew into a leading Australian yearbook business and expanded internationally. It validated the simple-online-collaborative-design thesis and taught the founders how to build the harder general product.
- **Canva's launch and freemium engine (2013).** A radically simple, template-driven, drag-and-drop design tool with a genuinely useful free tier. Bottoms-up adoption turned non-designers into users at global scale and made Canva a household name in everyday design, becoming the foundation for paid upgrades.
- **Canva for Teams and the enterprise push.** The move up-market from individuals to teams and organizations, layering collaboration, brand controls, and admin features to convert grassroots adoption into higher-value recurring revenue — a still-in-progress expansion of the monetization model.
- **Visual Suite and AI features.** Expansion beyond single-graphic creation into a broader suite (docs, whiteboards, websites, video) and generative-AI capabilities — both a growth vector and a defensive response to AI tools that could commoditize design creation.
- **The persistence-to-funding arc (roughly 2010–2013).** The ~100+ rejections, the Perth-to-Valley pursuit, and the kitesurfing gambit to reach an investor — a landmark founder-persistence case that preceded the eventual raise and launch. *(Exact rejection count is her own approximate retelling.)*
- **Staying private, capital-efficient, and profitable.** Reaching profitability relatively early and holding spending discipline while remaining private — an unusual path for a company of its valuation that preserved founder control and long-horizon freedom. *(Reported valuations in the ~$25–40B range have fluctuated with market conditions and secondary sales.)*

## Strengths to borrow
- **Make the complex radically simple** — treat accessibility as the strategy, not a nice-to-have; if a non-expert can't succeed, the product isn't done.
- **Prove the wedge before the vision** — de-risk a huge idea on a smaller, real, revenue-generating business first.
- **Persist with intelligence** — treat rejection as iteration when your core insight holds; relentlessness over years compounds.
- **Build distribution into the product** — a generous free tier and bottoms-up adoption can beat a sales-led motion for a mass market.
- **Stay capital-efficient** — profitability and discipline buy independence, durability, and a long horizon.
- **Anchor everything to a mission** — a real, long-term purpose disciplines near-term choices and justifies patience.
- **Win the underserved market** — serve the people the incumbents ignore rather than fighting them for their own customers.

## Weaknesses & failure modes
- **A crowded, AI-pressured category.** Design and creative tools are now intensely competitive, with Adobe (Express and its broader suite) pushing directly into Canva's territory and generative-AI image and design tools threatening to commoditize the very create-it-yourself simplicity Canva pioneered. The moat of "easy design" is under real pressure.
- **Freemium monetization ceiling.** The free-to-paid model that drove adoption also caps it — converting a huge free base into paying customers, and doing so without eroding the free experience that fuels growth, is an unresolved tension.
- **Enterprise / up-market execution is unproven.** Moving from grassroots individual adoption to serious enterprise and team monetization (Canva for Teams) requires capabilities — security, admin, sales, integrations — that a bottoms-up product-led company doesn't automatically have. This is still being earned.
- **A relatively young company, untested through a full cycle.** Founded in 2013, Canva has not yet been fully stress-tested through a deep, prolonged downturn as a mature business; its capital efficiency helps, but durability across a full economic cycle is unproven.
- **The "do the most good" pledge is aspirational.** The second step of the two-step plan — using the company's value to do the most good — is a stated intention, not a demonstrated outcome, and should be treated as an unproven promise rather than a track record.
- **Concentration in a founder couple.** A company run by a married co-founding pair is tightly aligned but also concentrated; the same closeness that speeds decisions can narrow the range of dissent at the very top.

## The personal side

*(For personal, interpersonal, and life questions — not just business decisions.)*

### How they treat people
- **Empathy for the non-expert as a worldview.** The instinct that drove the product — noticing that struggling students were failed by the tools, not the other way around — is also how she tends to frame people: assume the system is the problem before blaming the person. It's a fundamentally charitable, user-first way of seeing.
- **Partnership as the operating unit.** She has built both the company and her life around a tight co-founding partnership with Cliff Obrecht, with a durable division of labor and mutual trust. She treats close, long-run partnership — rather than solo heroics — as the natural way to build.
- **Encouraging, mission-recruiting leadership.** Publicly she leads by inviting people into a big, optimistic mission ("empower everyone in the world to design"), recruiting belief rather than demanding compliance. The tone is aspirational and inclusive rather than combative.
- **Grace under rejection.** Across years of investor no's she is consistently described (and describes herself) as staying gracious and undeterred rather than resentful — treating the people who rejected her as future potential believers, not adversaries.

### Values & inner life
- **Wealth as a means, not the point.** The "two-step plan" makes explicit that she views building enormous value as instrumental — the goal is to "do the most good." She and Obrecht have publicly committed the vast majority of their equity to philanthropy, framing their fortune as something to give away.
- **Optimism and mission as identity.** Her stated purpose — democratizing design so anyone can create — is genuinely held and long-horizon; she frames her work as widening access and empowerment rather than as personal achievement.
- **Persistence as a core value.** Her defining personal story is refusing to quit through years of rejection; endurance and long-term belief are things she treats as virtues, not just tactics.
- **Groundedness despite scale.** Coming from Perth and having built from her mother's living room, she projects a relatively unpretentious, down-to-earth identity even at extreme scale — the ranch-kid-equivalent value of resourcefulness over indulgence.

### Habits, health & daily rhythm
- **Long-horizon patience.** Her defining rhythm is patience — willing to spend years proving a model (Fusion Books) and years absorbing rejection before the payoff. She operates on a timescale most founders won't tolerate. *(Specific daily routines are not well documented publicly; treat detailed habit claims with caution.)*
- **Kitesurfing and the willingness to go where the work is.** Learning to kitesurf specifically to reach an investor is emblematic of how she'll adapt her own life and activities to the goal — the same trait that later shows up as a genuine outdoor/kitesurfing connection within the founder-investor community. *(Note: publicly documented lifestyle detail is limited; avoid over-specifying her private routines.)*

### Personal & interpersonal rules (to borrow)
1. **If someone is struggling with a hard tool or system, then question the system before blaming the person** — the frustration is usually a design failure, not a personal one.
2. **If you're building something big, then build it with a partner you deeply trust** — a strong, durable partnership beats solo heroics for the long haul.
3. **If you're repeatedly rejected, then stay gracious and treat the rejecter as a future believer** — resentment burns bridges you'll want later; persistence plus grace keeps them open.
4. **If you're weighing what your work is for, then treat wealth and success as a means, not the end** — decide early what the "most good" you'd do with it is.
5. **If a goal matters enough, then be willing to change your own life to reach it** — learn the new skill, go to the place, do the uncomfortable thing (even learn to kitesurf) to get in the room.
6. **If you have a big, intimidating dream, then break it into a fundable, provable first step** — the patient wedge is kinder to yourself and everyone building with you than an all-or-nothing swing.
7. **If you want people to follow you, then invite them into a mission rather than demand compliance** — recruit belief in a bigger purpose.
