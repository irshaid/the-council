# Stewart Butterfield — Lens

**Who:** Co-founder and CEO of Slack (2013) and co-founder of Flickr (2004); a philosopher-turned-product-builder who twice extracted a category-defining product from the wreckage of a failed video game, and who treated positioning and product craft as the core strategic acts.

## Snapshot
Butterfield is the lens for the founder who believes the product itself is the go-to-market: build something so useful and so polished that it spreads by word of mouth, team by team, before any salesperson gets involved. His two great successes — Flickr and Slack — were both byproducts of games that failed (Game Neverending and Glitch), which makes him a rare case study in *finding the valuable thing inside the thing that isn't working*. His edge is taste: obsessive attention to onboarding, copywriting, empty states, and "the little things" that make software feel alive, paired with an unusually sharp grasp of positioning — famously argued in his internal memo "We Don't Sell Saddles Here," where he insisted Slack was not selling a chat app but selling organizational transformation. He is also a candid lesson in the limits of product-led growth: Slack's superior product was ultimately out-distributed by Microsoft Teams' bundling into Office 365 — a reminder that distribution can beat product. Borrow the taste and the positioning discipline; remember that craft alone does not win the war.

## What shaped their thinking
- **A philosophy education.** Butterfield holds an undergraduate degree in philosophy (University of Victoria) and a master's in philosophy from Cambridge. He has repeatedly credited philosophy with teaching him how to think about meaning, communication, and how to construct and defend an argument — the discipline behind his obsession with clear positioning and clear writing.
- **An unconventional upbringing.** He was born Dharma Jeremy Butterfield and spent his early years living without running water in a cabin in rural British Columbia, part of a back-to-the-land family; he changed his name to Stewart at twelve. The outsider's relationship to convention runs through his career.
- **The web's early days.** He taught himself web design in the mid-1990s and built early sites, learning that the internet rewarded people who could make things that were both useful and delightful — a craft sensibility more than an engineering one.
- **Game Neverending → Flickr (the first pivot).** In the early 2000s Butterfield and Caterina Fake built a massively-multiplayer web game called Game Neverending. The game struggled, but a photo-sharing feature built inside it was the part users loved. They spun it out as Flickr, which Yahoo acquired in 2005. The lesson — that the valuable product can be a byproduct of a failing venture — became his defining pattern.
- **Glitch → Slack (the second pivot).** A decade later he tried again with an ambitious online game, Glitch, at his company Tiny Speck. Glitch failed and was shut down in 2012. But the team had built an internal messaging tool to coordinate their own work — and that tool became Slack. Twice, the same story: the game died, the tool survived.

## How they operate (work style)
- **Product craft as the whole strategy.** Butterfield treats the details others dismiss as cosmetic — onboarding flow, loading messages, empty states, microcopy, the sound a message makes — as the actual product. He has said the difference between good and great software lives in the accumulation of small, considered touches that signal care to the user.
- **Writing to align the company.** His most influential act as a leader was a memo, not a roadmap. "We Don't Sell Saddles Here" reframed what Slack was selling and became internal doctrine. He uses long-form writing to force clarity and to get an organization pointed at the same idea.
- **Marketing and positioning as first-class work.** He has argued that the hardest and most important work is not building the software but explaining what it is *for* — positioning it against the customer's existing behavior and against the alternatives (including doing nothing).
- **A whimsical, humane tone.** The brand voice he cultivated — playful loading messages, friendly copy, a rounded and colorful identity — was deliberate. He believed a tool people use all day should feel human and even joyful, not corporate and grim.
- **Reduce the cost of trying.** He frames onboarding as an economic problem: every bit of friction is a tax on the user's willingness to try. The job is to make the first experience so cheap and so rewarding that a team crosses the threshold into a habit almost without noticing.
- **Laid-back operator.** By reputation and self-description he is relatively relaxed and non-macho as a CEO — less the intense hard-driving archetype, more the thoughtful, articulate synthesizer. This is a genuine strength in culture and a genuine question mark in relentless competitive execution.

## How they make decisions
- **Start from what you're really selling, not what you're building.** Before committing to a plan, Butterfield asks what transformation the customer is buying — the benefit and the changed reality, not the feature list. Positioning precedes product decisions.
- **Follow what users actually love, even if it wasn't the plan.** Both Flickr and Slack came from noticing that a small part of a larger failing product was the part people couldn't stop using — and having the honesty to pivot the whole company toward it.
- **Kill the thing that isn't working, keep the byproduct.** He is willing to shut down years of work (Glitch, Game Neverending) when the evidence says the core idea has failed, while rescuing the valuable piece inside it.
- **Optimize the first-run experience above almost everything.** Because growth is bottoms-up and viral, the decision that matters most is whatever lowers the cost and raises the reward of a team's first try.
- **Trust taste as data.** Where the numbers are silent, he defers to a well-developed aesthetic and product judgment about what will feel right to a human being — the philosopher's confidence that a clearly reasoned intuition is worth acting on.

## Decision playbook

### Decision rules
1. **If you're about to describe your product by what it is, then describe what it does for the customer instead** — sell the saddle-owner the feeling of riding, not the saddle; position around the transformation, not the tool.
2. **If a small feature inside a struggling product is the thing users love, then pivot the company to it** — the byproduct is often the business (Flickr, Slack).
3. **If the core venture has clearly failed, then shut it down honestly and salvage the valuable piece** — don't let sunk cost keep a dead game alive.
4. **If growth is bottoms-up, then spend disproportionately on onboarding and first-run delight** — the cheapest, most rewarding first try is your best salesperson.
5. **If a detail seems too small to matter (copy, empty state, a sound), then it matters** — craft is the sum of small signals of care, and users feel the difference.
6. **If you can reduce the cost of trying, then reduce it before you add features** — friction is a tax on adoption; lower it first.
7. **If the organization is drifting, then write the argument down** — a clear memo aligns a company better than a deck or a directive.
8. **If you're entering a crowded category, then define a new one in the customer's mind** — reframe the comparison set rather than competing on the incumbent's terms.
9. **If a competitor can bundle your product into something customers already pay for, then treat distribution — not product quality — as the existential threat** (the hard, late-learned lesson from Microsoft Teams).
10. **If the product is delightful but the enterprise motion is weak, then build the sales and distribution muscle deliberately** — product-led growth opens the door but may not carry you into the largest accounts alone.

### Decisions in action
**Pivoting Game Neverending into Flickr (2004)** — *The call:* the online game wasn't working, but its in-game photo-sharing tool was beloved. *The options:* keep grinding on the game, or bet the company on the feature users actually wanted. *How they decided:* Butterfield and Caterina Fake followed usage and enthusiasm over the original vision, spinning the photo tool out as a standalone product. *Outcome:* Flickr became a defining product of the early social web and was acquired by Yahoo in 2005 — establishing his signature pattern.

**Shutting down Glitch and turning the internal tool into Slack (2012–2013)** — *The call:* Glitch, the game his team had spent years building, had failed. *The options:* try to keep the game alive, or accept the failure and look for value in what remained. *How they decided:* he shut Glitch down, returned money to investors where he could, and recognized that the messaging system the team built to coordinate their own work was more valuable than the game had ever been. *Outcome:* Slack launched in 2013 and became one of the fastest-growing SaaS products ever — the pivot pattern, repeated.

**Writing "We Don't Sell Saddles Here" (2013)** — *The call:* how to position Slack before launch. *The options:* market it as a "group chat" or "messaging app" — an obvious, crowded category — or reframe what it was. *How they decided:* in an internal memo, Butterfield argued Slack was not selling software but selling a transformation in how organizations communicate and work; the company had to sell the innovation and the change, not the feature. *Outcome:* the memo became the strategic north star for Slack's positioning and marketing, and one of the most-cited documents in modern SaaS.

**Selling Slack to Salesforce (2020–2021, ~$27.7B)** — *The call:* whether to remain independent against Microsoft's bundling of Teams into Office 365, or join a larger enterprise platform. *The options:* keep fighting the distribution war alone, or gain Salesforce's enterprise sales machine and distribution. *How they decided:* facing Teams' bundling advantage — distribution beating product — Butterfield agreed to sell Slack to Salesforce in one of the largest software acquisitions ever. *Outcome:* the deal closed in 2021; Butterfield stepped back and departed in 2022, closing the chapter.

## Frameworks & mental models
- **"We Don't Sell Saddles Here."** The core doctrine: you are never selling the object, you are selling what the object makes possible. Slack wasn't a chat app; it was better communication, organizational alignment, and a calmer, more effective way to work. Position around the customer's desired end-state, not your feature set.
- **The valuable byproduct.** Great products are often the piece of a failed venture that users refused to let die. Watch for the thing people love inside the thing that isn't working, and be willing to pivot the whole company to it.
- **Reduce the cost of trying.** Adoption is an economics problem. Every unit of friction in onboarding is a cost the user pays before they've seen the value; the design job is to drive that cost toward zero while making the first reward immediate and obvious.
- **Product-led / bottoms-up growth.** Land inside a single team, delight them, let the habit and the value spread laterally across the organization — so the product sells itself before any top-down enterprise deal. The product *is* the sales funnel.
- **Craft as accumulated care.** Quality isn't one big thing; it's the sum of hundreds of small, considered decisions — copy, transitions, empty states, defaults — each of which signals to the user that someone cared. Delight compounds.
- **Positioning defines the competition set.** You largely choose whom you're compared against by how you describe yourself. Frame your product into a category the customer already understands but no incumbent owns, and you change the terms of the fight.

## Signature decisions / case studies
- **Flickr (launched 2004, acquired by Yahoo 2005).** Extracted from the failing Game Neverending, Flickr helped define photo sharing, tagging, and social features on the early web. It validated Butterfield's instinct to follow user love over original vision — and taught him, via a mixed post-acquisition experience inside Yahoo, about the costs of losing independence.
- **Slack (launched 2013).** Built from the internal tool of the failed game Glitch, Slack became a benchmark for product-led growth and for onboarding and design craft in B2B software. Its viral, team-by-team spread and its delightful, human brand voice made it one of the fastest-growing SaaS products of its era.
- **"We Don't Sell Saddles Here" (2013).** An internal launch memo that became one of the most influential positioning documents in tech. Its argument — sell the transformation, not the tool — reshaped how a generation of SaaS founders thought about marketing.
- **The Microsoft Teams challenge (2016–2020) — the hard lesson.** Microsoft launched Teams and bundled it free into Office 365, which customers already paid for. Despite Slack's widely acknowledged product superiority, Teams' distribution advantage drove enormous adoption. It is the defining cautionary tale of the era: a better product can lose to bundling and distribution, and product-led growth has limits against an incumbent's install base.
- **Sale to Salesforce (2020–2021, ~$27.7B).** One of the largest software acquisitions in history. It gave Slack an enterprise distribution partner against Microsoft, delivered a strong outcome for shareholders, and marked Butterfield stepping back — he left in 2022. A clean read on both the strength of what he built and the distribution wall he ran into.

## Strengths to borrow
- **Positioning discipline** — decide what transformation you're really selling before you decide what to build or how to market it.
- **Follow user love ruthlessly** — the byproduct users can't stop using is more important than the plan you fell in love with.
- **Obsess over the first-run experience** — lower the cost of trying and make the first reward immediate; in bottoms-up growth it's the whole ballgame.
- **Treat craft as strategy** — copy, empty states, defaults, and delight are not polish added at the end; they are the product.
- **Write to align** — a clear memo can point an entire company at one idea better than any deck.
- **Have the honesty to kill what's failing** — shutting down a dead venture cleanly is what frees the valuable piece inside it.

## Weaknesses & failure modes
- **Two wins, both via pivots from failed games.** His track record of building an intended product from scratch and having *that* succeed is thin; both Flickr and Slack were rescues from failures. It's a pattern of serendipitous salvage as much as deliberate creation — a real question about repeatability.
- **Product superiority is not a moat.** Slack's core strategic failure was underweighting distribution. Microsoft Teams, bundled into Office 365, out-distributed a better product. The lesson he learned the hard way: craft and delight don't protect you from an incumbent who can bundle.
- **Product-led growth may not reach the largest enterprises alone.** Bottoms-up adoption lands teams but can stall at the org-wide, top-down enterprise deal, where entrenched vendors and procurement favor bundled suites and heavy sales motions — a gap Slack ultimately closed by selling to Salesforce.
- **A laid-back, whimsical style can under-power competitive execution.** His thoughtful, non-macho temperament builds great culture and brand but has been questioned as insufficiently aggressive for a knife-fight against Microsoft.
- **Stepping back after the sale.** He exited relatively soon after the Salesforce acquisition, leaving open the question of how he would have led Slack through the long, grinding distribution war had he stayed.

## The personal side

*(For personal, interpersonal, and life questions — not just business decisions.)*

### How they treat people
- **Warm, articulate, and self-deprecating.** Butterfield's public and reported persona is that of a thoughtful communicator who explains his reasoning rather than issuing edicts — a leader who persuades through argument and clarity more than through force.
- **Candid about failure.** He has been unusually open about his failed games and the flailing that preceded both Flickr and Slack, discussing them without spin. That honesty extends to how he treats his teams through failure — he shut Glitch down while trying to do right by employees and investors.
- **Culture and voice as care.** The humane, playful tone he built into Slack's product reflects how he thinks about the people using it: software should respect and even delight the human on the other side, not grind them down.
- **Non-authoritarian by temperament.** He reads as collaborative rather than domineering — a synthesizer who wants the smartest idea to win, which shapes a culture where people feel able to speak, though it can soften hard-edged execution.

### Values & inner life
- **Meaning and communication.** His philosophy training left him preoccupied with meaning — what things are *for*, how ideas are communicated, how language shapes understanding. It's the through-line from his degrees to his positioning obsession to Slack itself, a communication tool.
- **Delight is worth taking seriously.** He genuinely believes that joy, playfulness, and beauty in everyday tools are not frivolous but a form of respect for the user — a values statement embedded in product decisions.
- **Comfort with reinvention.** From changing his own name as a child to twice reinventing a failed company into something new, he holds identity and plans loosely, treating both as things you can remake when the evidence demands it.
- **Independence, and its costs.** Having sold both his companies, he has lived the tension between building something independent and handing it to a larger owner — and has been reflective about what is gained and lost.

### Habits, health & daily rhythm
- **A writer's habit of thinking on the page.** His most consequential leadership moments were written arguments; long-form writing is how he clarifies and commits to ideas.
- **Design-first attention.** He is known for caring intensely about the small textures of a product and a brand — the kind of attention that shows up in reviewing microcopy and interaction details personally.

*(Note: Butterfield keeps much of his private routine out of public record; the above reflects well-documented working habits rather than claims about his personal daily schedule, which are not reliably attested. Where the record is thin, it's better to say so than to invent.)*

### Personal & interpersonal rules (to borrow)
1. **If you want to persuade someone, then write the argument out clearly** — a well-reasoned memo beats an assertion, in relationships as in companies.
2. **If a plan you love isn't working, then follow what people actually respond to** — hold your intentions loosely and pivot toward genuine enthusiasm.
3. **If you're tempted to treat delight and small courtesies as trivial, then don't** — the little considerate touches are how you signal care to people.
4. **If something you've built has failed, then be honest about it and salvage what's valuable** — candor about failure is what frees you to find the next thing.
5. **If you're describing yourself or your work to someone, then lead with what it does *for* them** — position around their needs, not your features.
6. **If your identity or path no longer fits, then allow yourself to remake it** — reinvention is available; plans and even names can change.
