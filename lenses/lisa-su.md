# Lisa Su — Lens

**Who:** Chair and CEO of AMD (CEO since 2014); the MIT-trained semiconductor engineer who took AMD from the edge of bankruptcy to a high-performance computing powerhouse rivaling Intel and competing with NVIDIA, on the strength of a focused multi-year roadmap and disciplined execution.

## Snapshot
Su is the rare CEO who is also a deep technical operator — a PhD device physicist who understands the transistors, not just the P&L. When she took over AMD in 2014, the stock was around $2, the company was losing badly to Intel, and bankruptcy was a live possibility. She is a useful lens for a leader because her turnaround was not a single lucky product; it was a *decision discipline*: choose the hardest, highest-value problems, focus ruthlessly instead of chasing breadth, commit to a long technical roadmap, and then execute it quarter after quarter with almost no room for error. The "Zen" CPU architecture and the chiplet/modular design bet — treating chip design as a manufacturing-economics problem — were the concrete expressions of that discipline. Borrow the focus and the execution cadence; be honest that she is still trailing NVIDIA in AI, especially on the software moat, and that the turnaround was helped by timing and Intel's stumbles as well as skill.

## What shaped their thinking
- **Taiwan-born immigrant, engineering household.** Su was born in Tainan, Taiwan, and moved to the United States as a child. She was pointed toward math and engineering early and has spoken about a family culture that valued rigor and hard problems — a foundation for a career spent running toward difficulty rather than around it.
- **MIT, all the way to the transistor.** She earned her bachelor's, master's, and PhD in electrical engineering at MIT, with doctoral work on semiconductor devices (silicon-on-insulator technology). This is the root of her identity: she leads as an engineer who genuinely understands the physics, which lets her interrogate roadmaps and technical trade-offs at a level most CEOs cannot.
- **IBM and the technologist's apprenticeship.** At IBM she worked on advanced semiconductor R&D — notably helping push copper interconnect into mainstream chip manufacturing, a hard materials-and-process problem — and rose into a technical-strategic role advising the CEO. She learned to translate deep technology into business consequences.
- **Freescale and the operator's schooling.** Before AMD she ran businesses at Freescale Semiconductor, including networking and multimedia, and lived through the operational grind of shipping product on time in a brutally cyclical industry. She arrived at AMD having already learned execution the hard way.
- **The 2012–2014 near-death of AMD.** Su joined AMD in 2012 and became CEO in 2014 when the company was near collapse — dependent on stagnating PC markets, out-executed by Intel, and doubted by investors. The formative lesson of her tenure was made in that fire: a company with limited resources survives only by focusing everything on a few bets it can actually win.

## How they operate (work style)
- **Engineer-first leadership.** Su stays close to the technology. She reviews roadmaps in technical detail, asks about the hard engineering trade-offs, and is comfortable in the substance — which means teams can't hide weak technical logic behind business framing.
- **Run toward the hardest problems.** Her stated operating instinct is to move *toward* the biggest, hardest, highest-value problems rather than the easy adjacent wins. She frames the toughest markets (data center, high-performance compute, AI) as the ones worth the fight precisely because they are hard.
- **Focus over breadth.** With constrained resources she refused to spread AMD thin. The turnaround was built on picking a small number of bets — high-performance CPUs, then GPUs and data-center accelerators — and starving the distractions. "Say no to more things" is the operating reality behind the roadmap.
- **The multi-year roadmap as a contract.** Su runs AMD on public, multi-generation roadmaps (Zen 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 5, and the Instinct MI accelerator cadence). The roadmap is a commitment device: it forces internal prioritization and tells customers and partners exactly what to expect and when.
- **Metrics and execution discipline.** She is calm, rigorous, and relentlessly execution-driven. The emphasis is less on visionary rhetoric than on hitting the milestone, on time, generation after generation — because in semiconductors a single slipped node or missed launch window can be fatal.
- **Long lead times, patient conviction.** She thinks in the real cadence of silicon: bets made today ship in three to five years. That horizon shapes how she allocates — she is willing to be doubted for years while a roadmap compounds.

## How they make decisions
- **Value first: is this a big enough problem?** Su screens opportunities by the size and importance of the problem, not the ease of the win. If a market is large, hard, and strategically central (data center, AI compute), it clears the first gate; small or easy is disqualifying for scarce resources.
- **Focus filter: can we actually win it?** After importance comes honest capability assessment — can AMD field a genuinely competitive product on a realistic roadmap? Bets that fail either test get cut so the winnable ones get everything.
- **Roadmap-anchored, not reactive.** Decisions are made against a multi-year architectural plan rather than in response to the latest competitor move. The plan absorbs new information but is not whipsawed by it.
- **Technically grounded conviction.** Because she understands the engineering, her conviction on a hard bet (chiplets, Zen) rests on a defensible technical thesis, not just optimism — which is what let her hold the line through years of skepticism.
- **Manage the downside in a thin-margin business.** She is acutely aware that AMD historically had little room for error — dependence on TSMC capacity, tight cash, a larger rival. So decisions weigh execution risk heavily: sequencing, supply, and the cost of a slip, not just the upside.

## Decision playbook

### Decision rules
1. **If a problem is hard, large, and strategically central, then run toward it** — the toughest, highest-value markets are the ones worth fighting for; easy adjacent wins rarely change your trajectory.
2. **If resources are constrained, then focus beats breadth** — pick the few bets you can actually win and starve everything else; a weak company dies from spreading thin, not from saying no.
3. **If you're choosing a bet, then screen for importance first, winnability second** — a big problem you can't win is a trap; a small problem you can win is a distraction.
4. **If you're committing a direction, then publish a multi-year roadmap** — a public, multi-generation plan forces internal prioritization and turns customer trust into a durable asset.
5. **If a competitor moves, then check it against the roadmap before reacting** — absorb the information, but don't let the latest headline whipsaw a plan measured in years.
6. **If a hard engineering trade-off is the crux, then get into the substance yourself** — decide on a defensible technical thesis, not a slide; conviction that survives years of doubt has to be grounded.
7. **If the conventional design hits a cost or yield wall, then re-architect the economics** — attack the manufacturing math (chiplets/modularity), not just the transistor count.
8. **If you're in a thin-margin, single-foundry-dependent business, then weight execution risk heavily** — sequencing, supply, and the cost of a slipped node can matter more than the theoretical upside.
9. **If a bet ships in 3–5 years, then be willing to be doubted for 3–5 years** — hold conviction through the skepticism a long silicon horizon guarantees.
10. **If you're trailing a dominant incumbent, then compete where their moat is thinnest first** — win the hardware and price/performance battle you can win now, and build toward the harder moat (software/ecosystem) over time.

### Decisions in action
**Betting the company on "Zen" (decided ~2012–2014, shipped 2017)** — *The call:* whether to invest scarce resources in a clean-sheet, high-performance x86 CPU architecture when AMD was near bankruptcy and its existing cores were uncompetitive. *The options:* keep patching an inferior architecture and defend low-margin niches, or commit to a multi-year ground-up redesign that had to leapfrog Intel to matter. *How they decided:* importance-first (the high-performance CPU market was the only prize big enough to save the company) plus a defensible technical thesis (a new core plus a modular path could close the gap). *Outcome:* Zen launched in 2017 as Ryzen and EPYC, restored AMD's competitiveness, and became the foundation of the turnaround.

**The chiplet / modular architecture bet** — *The call:* how to build competitive, high-core-count CPUs without the collapsing yields and cost of ever-larger monolithic dies. *The options:* keep scaling monolithic chips (simpler, but expensive and yield-limited at advanced nodes), or split the design into small "chiplets" connected by a fabric. *How they decided:* re-architect the manufacturing economics — smaller dies yield better and cost less, letting AMD mix process nodes and scale core counts faster than a monolithic rival. *Outcome:* chiplets became a defining AMD advantage in servers (EPYC), a genuine cost-and-scaling breakthrough that Intel took years to answer.

**Going all-in on the data center and AI accelerators (Instinct MI300)** — *The call:* how aggressively to push AMD into data-center GPUs and AI training/inference against a dominant NVIDIA. *The options:* stay focused on the winning CPU franchise, or commit heavily to a hard, capital-intensive fight for AI compute. *How they decided:* run toward the hardest, highest-value market — AI compute is the biggest prize in the industry, so it clears the importance gate even though winning is far from assured. *Outcome:* the Instinct MI300 line gave AMD a credible data-center AI accelerator and real revenue, though AMD remains well behind NVIDIA, especially on software.

**Confronting the CUDA/software gap (ongoing, ROCm)** — *The call:* how to attack NVIDIA's real moat, which is the CUDA software ecosystem, not just the silicon. *The options:* compete mainly on hardware price/performance, or invest heavily to make AMD's ROCm software stack a viable alternative developers will adopt. *How they decided:* compete first where the moat is thinnest (hardware, price/performance, memory capacity) while investing in the harder, slower software fight. *Outcome:* AMD wins hardware comparisons on specific workloads but the software ecosystem gap remains the central unsolved problem of its AI strategy — the most honest limit of the turnaround.

## Frameworks & mental models
- **Run toward the hard problems.** Her core operating heuristic: the biggest, hardest, most important problems are the ones worth solving, because they're where durable value and defensibility live. Ease is a warning sign, not an attraction.
- **Focus as a survival strategy.** For a resource-constrained challenger, focus is not a preference but a condition of survival — pick the few winnable, high-value bets and refuse the rest. Breadth is how weak companies die.
- **The multi-year roadmap.** Treat the roadmap as both an internal prioritization engine and an external trust contract: commit publicly to multi-generation plans, then execute them on cadence so customers can build on your word.
- **Execution discipline / the cadence.** In semiconductors, strategy is only as good as the ability to hit the node, the launch window, and the performance target generation after generation. Reliability of execution *is* the strategy.
- **Re-architect the economics.** When conventional scaling hits a physical or cost wall, change the structure of the problem (chiplets/modularity) rather than pushing harder on the same approach — solve the manufacturing math, not just the engineering.
- **Long-horizon conviction.** Decisions play out over the 3–5 year lead time of silicon, so hold conviction through extended doubt; the payoff is measured in generations, not quarters.

## Signature decisions / case studies
- **The AMD turnaround (2014–present).** Taking over a company near bankruptcy — stock around $2, out-executed by Intel — Su led one of the great corporate turnarounds, rebuilding AMD into a high-performance computing company whose market value and product competitiveness came to rival Intel's. The mechanism was focus plus roadmap execution, not a single miracle product.
- **Zen (2017).** The clean-sheet CPU architecture that restored AMD's competitiveness in both consumer (Ryzen) and server (EPYC) markets, and anchored every subsequent generation. The foundational bet of the turnaround.
- **Chiplets / EPYC in the data center.** The modular design approach let AMD scale core counts and cost-efficiency faster than Intel's monolithic designs, driving real server-market share gains — a manufacturing-economics breakthrough as much as an engineering one.
- **Instinct MI300 and the AI push.** AMD's credible entry into data-center AI accelerators, generating meaningful revenue and giving hyperscalers an NVIDIA alternative — while remaining clearly the number-two player, especially on software.
- **The NVIDIA/CUDA gap (the unfinished case).** The clearest limit of the turnaround: AMD's hardware is competitive on specific workloads, but NVIDIA's CUDA software ecosystem remains a moat AMD's ROCm has not closed. An honest scorecard credits the hardware progress and marks the software gap as still open.

## Strengths to borrow
- **Focus under constraint** — when resources are scarce, win by saying no to almost everything and pouring everything into the few bets you can actually take.
- **Run toward hard problems** — treat difficulty and market size as the signal of where durable value is, not as a reason to retreat.
- **Roadmap discipline** — commit to a multi-year plan publicly, then execute it on cadence so it becomes both an internal filter and an external trust asset.
- **Technical depth as leadership leverage** — understand the substance well enough to interrogate the real trade-offs; it makes conviction defensible and weak plans visible.
- **Calm, metrics-driven execution** — win by hitting the milestone reliably, generation after generation, not by rhetoric or heroics.
- **Re-architect the economics** — when the standard approach hits a wall, change the structure of the problem rather than pushing harder on the same path.

## Weaknesses & failure modes
- **Still number two in AI — especially on software.** AMD's biggest strategic gap is NVIDIA's CUDA ecosystem moat. Competitive hardware has not translated into ecosystem parity; ROCm adoption lags, and this remains the central unsolved problem of the AI strategy. Any AMD bull case has to survive this fact.
- **Heavy dependence on TSMC.** AMD is fabless and leans on TSMC for leading-edge manufacturing capacity. That concentrates enormous strategic risk in a single foundry and in the geopolitics of Taiwan — a vulnerability outside AMD's direct control.
- **The turnaround had tailwinds, not just skill.** Intel's manufacturing stumbles (its prolonged struggles at 10nm/7nm) and favorable timing gave AMD an opening. Su's execution was real and disciplined, but attributing the entire recovery to individual genius overstates it; some of the door was opened by a rival's failure.
- **Execution-dependent with thin room for error.** The model works because AMD executes the roadmap almost flawlessly. That same reliance means a slipped node, a missed launch window, or a supply shortfall could do outsized damage — the strategy has little slack.
- **Less proven as a category *creator*.** Su has excelled at out-executing incumbents in established high-value markets. Whether the same playbook can *create* a new category or overturn a competitor's ecosystem moat (as opposed to winning on hardware) is not yet demonstrated.

## The personal side

*(For personal, interpersonal, and life questions — not just business decisions.)*

### How they treat people
- **Calm, exacting, respectful.** Su is widely described as measured and even-keeled rather than volatile — she holds a very high bar but is not known for the theatrical intensity of some tech CEOs. The demand is high; the delivery is composed.
- **Credibility through competence.** Because she genuinely understands the engineering, she earns technical teams' respect by engaging with their work on the merits. Trust is built by being able to have the real conversation, not by title.
- **Straight talk and accountability.** Her leadership style leans on clear commitments and holding people (and herself) to them — the roadmap culture is also a culture of owning what you said you'd deliver.
- **A role model by presence.** As one of the most prominent women and Asian-American leaders in the semiconductor industry, she has been visible about the responsibility that comes with it, and about widening the field to more engineers.

### Values & inner life
- **Rigor and hard problems as a way of life.** The instinct to run toward difficulty is not just a business tactic; it reflects a personal preference for meaningful, demanding work over easy wins.
- **Long-term patience.** She is comfortable being doubted for years while a plan compounds — a temperament of delayed gratification suited to an industry with multi-year lead times.
- **Engineering identity.** She continues to identify as an engineer and technologist first; the technical substance is where her sense of the work seems to live, not the executive trappings.
- **Documented family tie to Jensen Huang.** Su and NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang are relatives (distant cousins) — a well-documented and much-noted fact given that AMD and NVIDIA are direct competitors. Both have acknowledged it publicly and matter-of-factly.

### Habits, health & daily rhythm
- **Deep in the details.** By repeated account she works hard and stays unusually hands-on with product and roadmap reviews for a CEO of her scale — the engagement with substance is a defining daily habit rather than an occasional deep dive.
- **Composure as a discipline.** Her calm public demeanor under competitive and financial pressure reads as a cultivated operating style: steady, prepared, low-drama.

*(Note: Su is relatively private about personal routines, diet, and health; specific daily habits are less documented than for some peers, so this section is deliberately limited to what is well attested.)*

### Personal & interpersonal rules (to borrow)
1. **If a problem looks hard, then lean in rather than away** — in work and in life, the hard, meaningful problems are usually the ones worth your time.
2. **If you want a team's respect, then earn it on the merits** — engage with the substance of people's work; credibility comes from competence, not authority.
3. **If you've made a commitment, then treat it as a contract** — say what you'll deliver and deliver it; reliability compounds into trust.
4. **If you're tempted to react to every provocation, then stay composed** — steadiness under pressure is a discipline that outperforms drama.
5. **If you're spread too thin, then say no to more things** — focus is as much a personal practice as an organizational one.
6. **If success comes slowly, then be patient with the long horizon** — meaningful things compound over years; don't abandon a good plan for lack of quick payoff.
7. **If you hold a visible position, then use it to widen the door** — model the path for the people who don't yet see themselves in the room.
